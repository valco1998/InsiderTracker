What happens in the six months after insiders buy together?

We found every case where two or more company insiders bought the same stock within a short window for more than $250,000 combined. We then followed each stock for 180 days, from the purchase date in hindsight and from the moment the filings became public, and compared it with similar stocks and with SPY.

Cluster stocks often rose at some point, but no more than similar stocks did, and less than SPY over the same six months.

Half of the clusters touched +20% at some point within 180 days, and the median peak was about +24%. Similar stocks chosen the same day ended the 180 days at about the same place (−1.3 points, not significant). They also peaked higher and reached +20% more often, but that is because cluster stocks became calmer after the purchases, not because insider buying capped the upside. The 6.6-point shortfall against SPY is shared by every stock with insider filings in this period, so it is not specific to clusters. Bigger purchases did not help. More insiders gave a weak positive hint that did not survive the multiple-testing correction. The clearest finding is that the public sees the filings after part of the move: buying at the trade-date close instead of after disclosure would have added about 4.4 points over 180 days, most of it in the few days between the trades and the filing. All six frozen strategies were rejected. The sample is 108 public events in one market period (Dec 2025 – Nov 2026), so this is not strong evidence against clusters in general. It is enough evidence not to trade them now.

Clock 2 · public results start at the official open after the SEC filings that completed the cluster became public, plus 60 seconds. Clock 1 · hindsight results start at the close of the trade date that completed the cluster, before anyone outside the company knew. Peaks are the highest daily high reached. A touch is not a profit you could have locked in, because nobody knows in advance which day is the peak.

Frozen strategies

These rules were fixed in advance and exits were never chosen from the observed peak. Each was measured on Dec–Feb entries (design), then Mar–early May entries (confirmation, 180 days), then later entries at 90 days (extension). The periods overlap in calendar time and share one market, so a pass would only have justified paper trading.

Answers

Limits and the data that would change the answer

  • One market period. All complete windows fall between Dec 2025 and Nov 2026, which is about 1.9 non-overlapping 180-day periods. SPY gained about 12% over the average window, so a broad rally can hide or create effects.
  • Comparison universe. Prices are cached only for the 2,428 tickers that had an insider purchase at some point, not the whole market. Similar stocks come from tickers whose first purchase filing was already public, which removes the look-ahead flaw found in study 4. This group still differs from the whole market, so the SPY comparison is the cleaner benchmark for "beat the market".
  • No market cap or sector. Size is approximated by dollar volume and price, and sector matching was not possible.
  • Survivorship. 20 cluster tickers have no cached prices (delisted, funds, OTC) and are left out. Three series end inside their window and carry their last close.
  • Prices are split-adjusted only. Dividends are not included, which understates the return of high-yield funds and banks a little.
Proposed data, not downloaded and awaiting your approval:
  1. SEC Form 4 history for 2016–2025 (the SEC's free quarterly insider-transaction data sets). This would give roughly 40 quarters of clusters across several market regimes, enough for a real holdout. The bulk files may give only filing dates, not exact acceptance times; that needs checking before relying on them for the public clock.
  2. Daily prices for every listed stock, including delisted ones, for the same years. This allows unbiased similar-stock matching over the whole market. The current Alpaca account covers listed symbols; delisted names would need a source that keeps them.
  3. Shares outstanding and SIC sector codes from SEC data (free). These allow true market-cap and sector matching, and the "purchase vs company size" question.
